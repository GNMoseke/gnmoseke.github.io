This post was heavily inspired by my good friend Lane's retrospective last year, which you can find
[here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fNI6yWwa8QQ). She has an actual degree in this stuff, so her takes have a lot more thought put into

I play a lot of video games (which I'm sure comes as quite a shock - a mid-20's guy playing lots of games? Unheard of). Not as many as I did
before I had to do lame non-video-game things like *work* and *cook* and *be a generally functional adult*, but I still find time to get
through a solid dozen or so every year. Games are really special to me. They're the catalyst for my becoming a software engineer, they
offered a community for me to be a part of in high school, and they're how I stay connected with my friends now that we live on completely
different continents. Just like a good book, a good game has the ability to pretty much rule your every waking thought for days on end. This
post is a love letter to the things that did that for me in 2024.

A quick reference on my taste so that you're not going in blind. I won't be assigning any of the games I played (or
:counterclockwise_arrows_button: replayed) this year a rating, but I'll of course talk about what I liked and didn't like. It feels only
fair to give you the short list of games that I **personally** consider to be 10/10:
* [Hades](https://www.supergiantgames.com/games/hades/), my favorite game of all time and nothing else is even close.
* [Dishonored 2](https://bethesda.net/en/game/dishonored2), which is my favorite *world* in a game I've played (and the game itself kicks ass).
* [Hellblade: Senua's Sacrifice](https://www.hellblade.com/), the best acted game I've ever played and a perfect example of "longer is not necessarily better."
* [The Last of Us/The Last of Us Part II](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/the-last-of-us/), grouped together because they're one absolutely gripping story (and are close on the heels
  of Hellblade for acting prowess).

You should play all of those games, if you haven't.

The list of games I played this year is (mostly) chronological, so let's jump right in with what took up most of my January:

## Hogwarts Legacy
{{< figure
  src="https://cdn-hogwartslegacy.warnerbrosgames.com/media/screen-05.jpg"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.hogwartslegacy.com/en-us/media)"
>}}

I fucking love Harry Potter. Let's just get that out of the way. It was a huge part of my childhood and it's somewhat of a comfort food
franchise for me as an adult. I put off buying Hogwarts Legacy for almost a year, for various reasons. But after it went on sale during the
2023 holidays, and having just moved to a brand new country over 8000km from my friends and family, I needed something unobtrusive and
comforting to play while getting used to the... *ahem* "joys" of the Dutch winter (translation: I did not get to see the sun for weeks). So
I caved and bought the game.

I will say one thing immediately for Hogwarts Legacy: Hogwarts is near perfect. Avalanche Studios deserves every bit of praise they've
received for how reactive and alive the castle feels. I had a huge grin on my face for most of the first few hours just running around
exploring and finding my classes. Hogsmeade, too, is excellent. It feels exactly like a kooky wizard village should. Every minute of
the first 10-15 hours is an absolute blast, especially because I also loved the combat system - it felt like I was actually flinging out
spells on the fly, and the combos you can cook up were a blast.

Unfortunately, the game stumbles anywhere outside the bounds of the castle and village. Don't get me wrong, flying over the Scottish
countryside to find a tucked away dungeon or hamlet is fun at first. And then you do it about 300 more times. I get it, they wanted to go
big and went with the open world model, but it's just such a boring, copy-paste open world. The combat is a blast but when you're clearing
the same camps of dark wizards and spiders for hours on end it does leave you wanting something with just a little more flavor to it.

The story is so forgettable that I left it to the penultimate sentence. Hogwarts Legacy is not a bad game, but it's not a $60 one either.

## Disco Elysium
{{< figure
  src="https://discoelysium.com/_next/image?url=https%3A%2F%2Fres.cloudinary.com%2Fdisco-elysium%2Fimages%2Fw_2560%2Ch_1440%2Cc_scale%2Ff_auto%2Cq_auto%2Fv1607616920%2Fscreenshot_climbing%2Fscreenshot_climbing.jpg%3F_i%3DAA&w=3840&q=75"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://discoelysium.com/media)"
>}}

I've had friends tell me to play Disco Elysium for years, but I had always bounced off the art style a bit and I knew that it was much more
of an "interactive book" style game. I like that style, but it's a style that I have to be in the mood for, since it generally means I can't
just sit down and play for a few minutes at a time, I need to be fully engaged for at least an hour or two. But with my first winter in the
Netherlands hitting its peak and having nothing to look at outside other than dark gray misery, I figured I'd trade it for dark gray
*pixelated* misery with an oil painting vibe.

Holy shit, I should have played Disco Elysium sooner. The writing flavor and worldbuilding may be second to none in any game, maybe any piece of
media I've ever played (and that's saying something, especially with some of the other games I finished in 2024). Kim Kitsuragi in
particular may be the single most well-realized fictional character I've ever seen. I don't want to say too much about the story - even
though the game has been out for 5 years at this point I think that you do a disservice to yourself if you don't go in as blind as possible.

The pure artistry is what sets Disco Elysium apart to me. It, more than any other narrative game I've adored, feels like a very personal
labor of love. I don't think the gameplay itself is perfect by any means, but the game just oozes character like nothing else. Highly
recommended.

## Bloodborne (Twice)
{{< figure
  src="https://images3.alphacoders.com/641/thumb-1920-641193.jpg"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/bloodborne/)"
>}}

After spending a couple weeks on a game that was primarily reading, I figured I'd change it up to a game that was primarily getting my ass
kicked. Much as it pains me to admit, Elden Ring was my first FromSoftware game, even though most of my friends have run the whole
soulsborne gamut. So I figured I'd start my education with the game that gave me the best vibe: Bloodborne. And after making the classic
mistake of thinking that I could play the DLC after finishing the main game first, I played it again a few weeks later.

This game has to be judged by Old Hunters. It adds 3 of the best bosses in the game (and a stinker but we don’t talk about the stinker).
Maria of the Astral Clocktower is one of the best bosses I have ever fought, and the environments especially in the middle of Old Hunters are
absolutely insane. Otherwise, Bloodborne starts and ends strong. Father Gascoigne is an absolutely fantastic boss fight, up there with my
favorites from Elden Ring. Similarly, on a visual spectacle level, Mergo's Wet Nurse is tied with Rykard for my favorite FromSoft boss (though it only
took me 2 tries to beat and I wish it was a tad more aggressive). Something in Bloodborne really clicked for me towards the end and I love
its much more aggression-based combat. It also has really fantastic looping level design - there were definitely a few spots where
checkpoints were annoyingly placed, but there were no incredibly punishing boss run-ups. The game also OOZES ambiance, "eldritch nightmare
victorian magastructures" has got to be one of the coolest themes I've played.

The only place where Bloodborne really faltered for me was in the middle - I can't even remember most of the bosses save for The One Reborn and Micolash
(the former because it was so over-the-top visually, the latter because he was annoying as shit). I think the world still draws me in but
the bosses are somewhat forgettable. Still, Bloodborne goes definitively on the "man, why didn't I play this sooner" pile.

## Sifu
{{< figure
  src="https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/61b314d453c56145299e8a65/621648ec0c4ac8b567e4bee0_SIFU_Reveal_Screenshot_1920x1080_Dancefloor-p-1600.jpeg"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.sifugame.com/gallery)"
>}}

One of the first non-kids movies I remember watching with my dad is [Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crouching_Tiger,_Hidden_Dragon).
So when a game was announced that was basically "Ok, now you get to do that," it went on my wishlist pretty much immediately.

Sifu is great. The combat system is polished to a shine, and executing a perfect sequence of blocks into a stagger break and then sweeping
combo of attacks is immensely satisfying, with the extra-crisp animations and sound cues really scratching that Kung Fu itch. The game is
also super pretty, especially (as you'll hear in basically every review) the nightclub stage. I honestly don't have much more to say about
*Sifu* - it did what it set out to do. It was a satisfying, gameplay-focused experience that was just punishing enough to have that moment
where it "clicks" and you become a walking flurry of fists and feet, while not overstaying its welcome or pulling out stupid tricks for
bosses; it's just a well-made game that knows what its strengths are and plays to them.

## Uncharted 4: A Thief's End :counterclockwise_arrows_button:
{{< figure
  src="https://gmedia.playstation.com/is/image/SIEPDC/uncharted-4-screenshot-16-15jun15?$native$"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/uncharted-4-a-thiefs-end/)"
>}}

To get the only negative out of the way at the get-go: I think this game is balanced around "normal" difficulty, and it very clearly shows
when you play it on hard/crushing. There's nothing wrong with a challenge, but the design of the combat system is clearly intended for the
normal difficulty and becomes slightly frustrating towards the end of the game when playing on a harder difficulty.

That said: Uncharted 4 is an absolute masterclass of story, environment, acting, and ambiance. It is the best parts of every Indiana Jones
movie mushed together, with characters that are written 10x better (minus Last Crusade) and set pieces that few other things I've played tops (especially for a
game that came out in 2016, this looks absolutely mind-bogglingly good). Every character feels real and whole and human, every conversation
feels like actual humans reacting the way they should (with maybe a touch of extra wit), and the absolute goddamn VARIETY of this game is truly
stunning. It feels like the perfect globe-trotting, gun slingin' adventure that was promised, and it does it without eye-rolling dialogue or
some of the slightly cheap supernatural elements that the first 3 games relied on. Uncharted 4 fires on all cylinders and doesn't overstay
its welcome, it is an absolute joy.

## Ori and the Blind Forest :counterclockwise_arrows_button:
{{< figure
  src="https://shared.cloudflare.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/387290/ss_d355fa04e0469134629eadcdef627cae490b8f8c.1920x1080.jpg?t=1701967651"
  caption="Image Credit: Moon Studios"
>}}

I’m actually quite sad to have replayed this, because I think it ruined what were very good memories of Ori and the Blind Forest - and I
think it will mean I don’t plan to replay Will of the Wisps for a while. Fundamentally, my opinion is that it is over-designed and they needed to stop
introducing new movement mechanics after Bash, because you never get quite enough time to get comfortable with them before you're asked to
master something new.

The art and music are great, the story is simple but touching enough, but the game becomes frustrating and irksome in the latter half, which is unfortunate.

## Red Dead Redemption 2
{{< figure
  src="https://media-rockstargames-com.akamaized.net/mfe6/prod/__common/img/3ee9139a4c4947e93284317a53d2a13e.jpg?im=Resize=1920"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.rockstargames.com/reddeadredemption2)"
>}}

An incredible narrative, tour de force in character writing, and masterclass in writing an antihero. The following paragraph will sound
mostly critical, but I want to make it clear that I'm intentionally nitpicking because the game is just that good.

I think this game, while the story was truly one of the best I've experienced, is held down by lackluster game design and a need to be both an
expansive, engaging open world and a linear narrative thrill ride, and that split-brain design philosophy makes both approaches suffer
needlessly. Either lean fully into the hand-holding approach, or let me do missions with some creative freedom, you can't try and have it
both ways. As a final point of critique, I think that the epilogue of the game actually kills some of the emotional hit of the ending of the
main story. Though I did enjoy the epilogue quite a bit, and I know it needs to bridge the gap between this game and the original, I felt
that the ending of Arthur Morgan's story was somewhat overshadowed by the beginning of John Marston's, and Arthur was really the character
that the game was built around.

All that said, I do truly think that this game is worth playing for the narrative alone; if you can look past the antiquated cover shooting
and somewhat repetitive mission structure (go here, talk to some person, shoot your way out), you'll find an absolute gem of writing that
rightfully deserves the praise heaped upon it. I don't want to talk too much about the finer points of the story (even though the game has been out for over 5
years at this point) because I truly think it deserves as fresh of an experience as possible. That said though, I was still thinking about
Red Dead Redemption 2 a couple of weeks after I had finished it and moved on to something else, and I think that speaks volumes about how
much of an impact the story had.

## Hollow Knight
{{< figure
  src="https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/606d159a953867291018f801/6cdafaab-e95a-49c7-a045-4f21469bb327/Blue_Cave.jpg?format=1500w"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.hollowknight.com/)"
>}}

Truly a painting come to life, the art and world in Hollow Knight are its crowing achievements. Every enemy is lovingly animated, every
scenic vista is a moment to stop and drink in the ambiance. The combat and platforming are razor tight and responsive, and every swing of the nail or dash of the cloak is snappy and satisfying. I
didn't find any of it to be particularly innovative: it knows what it is and sticks to it, which isn't a bad thing. 

My only relatively minor gripes are the lack of variety in interesting charms (once I had a good build I basically stuck to it for the whole
game) and the weird dream nail mechanics. Lore wise and in some spots the dream nail makes plenty of sense, but it doesn't really do
anything outside of those set interaction points; when it was given to me I thought it would be a more mechanically interesting alternative
to the normal nail and was assuming that there would be bosses or enemies that required its use, but that wasn't really the case. I would
have preferred it just be a normal interaction prompt instead. To be fair as well, my first playthrough was only 16 hours and 55%
completion, and I didn't touch the DLCs, so it certainly could take on some later game uses and I will absolutely revisit it on a replay.

That's my only real tangible complaint with the game though, every boss was fun, every enemy (except the stupid fucking Soul Sanctum
teleporty guys) was fun, and the music was absolutely enchanting. High marks, and will probably only gain them on a replay. **Insert joke
about Silksong never coming out here.**

## Hades II (Early Access)
{{< figure
  src="https://images.ctfassets.net/5owu3y35gz1g/28gMtYpCnTPfHNgGlDI1Ab/bd2d077b524763f8ae9d3d961464fe93/H2_Wallpaper_Melinoe_4k_01.png?w=1920&q=80"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.supergiantgames.com/games/hades-ii)"
>}}

Remember at the start of this blog when I said Hades is my favorite game of all time and nothing else is even close? Well, Hades II is
very quickly making that case for itself.

When Hades II came out in early access in May, I wasn't sure if I wanted to hop in right away or try and hold out for a 1.0 release. So obviously
the next day I bought a Steam Deck just to play it. And holy shit, is it good. I did not expect it to be as polished and full of content as
it was, even before the Olympic Update. It feels enough like the first game to instantly get you comfortable, but with just enough of a
twist on the combat flow with the new omega abilities that entirely new ways of playing open themselves up after just a few runs. It is
already everything I could have possibly hoped for and more, and ***it's still in early access.***

I can't gush enough about Hades II. It kicks so much ass. Give Supergiant all of your money, I can't think of a studio that deserves it
more.

## Spider-Man 2
{{< figure
  src="https://gmedia.playstation.com/is/image/SIEPDC/spider-man-2-screenshot-story-duo-4k-legal-13jul23?$2400px$"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/marvels-spider-man-2/)"
>}}

It's hard to sum up my thoughts about Spider-Man 2. On the one hand, I did really enjoy it - the movement is cranked up a notch from the
previous game (if that's even possible), the combat is fluid and fun and fast (as long as you're fighting a big mob of enemies, I felt the
bosses could be a touch annoying). The characters are well acted, the animation is superb, and the set pieces for some of the missions are a
particular standout. Rampaging through NYC as Venom, swinging through magic portals in pursuit of Black Cat, and the arenas for the nearly
hour-long final boss fight are all absolute spectacles that put your jaw on the floor.

Where Spider-Man 2 stumbles for me, unfortunately, is in its story and characters. The story isn't what I would call "bad" per say, it just
feels... weaker than it should. For something with such an emphasis on second chances, redemption, and forgiveness, it doesn't feel like any
of the characters ever earn it. They just correct a single mistake or say how sorry they are and then boom, forgiven. I was actually happy
when Miles straight up told Martin Li that he won't ever forgive him, but it was done in such a way that it felt like the game was saying
"Look! Mister Negative is complicated! Deep down he's a good guy, he was just driven by the wrong things!" And like, no. He is a
supervillain. He murdered a lot of people and destroyed large parts of the city. He is objectively a bad person.

As a last note, I wish the game had more of Miles in it. It felt like they set out to make a game about both Spider Men, but wrote a story
that was centered around Peter. That means that Miles gets sidelined for a good chunk of the game (or at least it felt like he did), and
what story he did have was pretty much exclusively focused around how he's trying to save Peter and make Peter better rather than who Miles
actually is as the younger, less sure of himself Spider Man.

To sum up my feelings, this is a strange case where I absolutely love the backdrop that a narrative is set against, and find myself
disliking the pieces that are set up within it. I will probably give this a replay, at least to do some of the side content I skipped the
first go around. And to replay some of the more insane set pieces, just to marvel at some of the stuff they throw at you.

## Horizon: Forbidden West
{{< figure
  src="https://gmedia.playstation.com/is/image/SIEPDC/horizon-forbidden-west-screenshot-18-en-02feb22?$2400px$"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.playstation.com/en-gb/games/horizon-forbidden-west/)"
>}}

A cool world tarnished by bad characters and an eye-rolling main quest. Let me fight robot megafauna in peace without cardboard cutout
NPCs delivering stupid dialogue constantly.

Look, that's maybe a bit harsh. I really enjoyed the first Horizon game - the worldbuilding is a really neat twist on a post-apocalyptic
setting and though I didn't find the narrative to be anything super amazing, it served to hold my interest and Aloy was an interesting
enough protagonist. Forbidden West tries to do the same thing, but the problem is that Aloy has already saved the world once, so the stakes
have to be upped in such an extreme way that it just didn't click with me. I don't think the main quest has bad bones. On the contrary, I
enjoyed the general setup and some of the villains. It just felt like it dropped too deeply into the "overdone sci-fi trope" end of the pool
instead of embracing the more interesting parts of the Zero Dawn program.

Forbidden West wasn't bad, but much like Hogwarts Legacy it felt like a game with a few really solid ideas/moments overshadowed by the need
to proudly proclaim itself "open world."

## Elden Ring :counterclockwise_arrows_button: + Shadow of the Erdtree
{{< figure
  src="https://gmedia.playstation.com/is/image/SIEPDC/elden-ring-shadow-of-the-erdtree-screenshot-02-en-28feb24?$2400px$"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/elden-ring/elden-ring-shadow-of-the-erdtree/)"
>}}

You don't need me to tell you that Elden Ring is a fantastic game. The entire internet has said so for almost 3 years now. It was my first
FromSoft game back when it came out, and Shadow of the Erdtree was the perfect excuse to run through it again, this time with Bloodborne
under my belt, a better understanding of how to play the game, and a desire to beat many of the bosses I had skipped on my first go around.

I blazed through most of the base game, which felt really good. The only boss to give me more than 20 minutes of trouble was (somewhat
fittingly) Mohg - but once he *nihil*-ed no more I hopped right into the Realm of Shadow. And promptly got my ass absolutely handed to me.
Dancing Lion took me an entire curse-laden evening, and it only got more brutal from there.

But man if they didn't knock it out of the park with the map design in the DLC. The vertical, looping design felt like there was something
new (and probably extremely lethal) around every bend, and most of my favorite parts of the DLC were just wandering around, seeing what
fresh new horrors or incredible vistas FromSoftware had cooked up. A particular highlight for me is the Shadow Keep, which felt like
something straight out of all of my favorite dark fantasy books.

The bosses were a bit hit or miss - I had an absolute blast against Rellana for example, but Commander Gaius may be my least favorite boss
in the entire game. Some bosses felt like they had a fair (if wildly difficult) phase one, only to break the bullshit-meter in the second
phase just because. I did eventually "git gud" as it were, and after 2 full weekends of fighting the final boss, I finally checked Shadow of
the Erdtree off my list. The most satisfying part, though, was destroying the base game's final boss in one try when I had never actually
beaten it in my first playthrough.

## God of War (2018) & God of War: Ragnarök :counterclockwise_arrows_button:
{{< figure
  src="https://gmedia.playstation.com/is/image/SIEPDC/god-of-war-ragnarok-watermark-screenshot-11-en-08sep21?$2400px$"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/god-of-war-ragnarok/)"

A masterclass in narrative, animation, acting, gameplay, music, everything. 

This was my second go around with both of the Norse-era God of War games, and I was happy to find that they both held up incredibly well on
replay, and I was just as invested as the first go. I think the first game lacks a bit in enemy variety, and the second has some tedious
puzzle elements, but on the whole those things don't bother me when I'm thrown into an absolute peak to peak thrill ride with tight writing,
amazing acting, awesome combat, 10/10 visuals, and some of the coolest bosses in any game I've played. To echo a quote from [Dunkey](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sbcrJ7PDmFA) (forgive me),

## Pyre
{{< figure
  src="https://images.ctfassets.net/5owu3y35gz1g/58JAPafczmggiimQ2wqiSs/c34007dc96d2b8574b91a84019113a2a/Pyre_02.jpg?w=1920&q=80"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.supergiantgames.com/games/pyre)"
>}}

I am ashamed to admit that as much as I love Hades (and now Hades II), I had never played the rest of the Supergiant catalog, even
though I had owned them for a while. So I decided, after the Olympus Patch for Hades II was released and I had re-scratched that itch, to
spend November playing through the first 3 Supergiant games starting with Pyre.

At first I just couldn't quite click with Pyre. I'd spend 20-30 minutes on it, interested in the characters but bouncing off of the
"basketball" that makes up most of the gameplay. It wasn't until I had unlocked a couple more of the Nightwings that I really started to
engage with the game all the way, and then I blasted through it (though I have to admit I was pretty single-strategy for most of it).

Pyre really shines in its characters and particularly in its music for me. Which shouldn't be a surprise coming from Supergiant (and you'll
hear me echo this exact sentiment with the next two games), but still it's always impressive just how immersive their stylized worlds can
be. It's a wonderful way to spend 10 hours, especially as a chill break from gigantic, sprawling AAA blockbusters.

## Bastion
{{< figure
  src="https://images.ctfassets.net/5owu3y35gz1g/3hJBK3oooUIIwwuS88kkWW/e24effd8175787877822647fa550be09/1408083058104_Bastion_PC_0004.jpg?w=1920&q=80"

Next up on my Supergiant gauntlet was their first game - Bastion. And it hooked me off the rip with Logan Cunningham's excellent southern
drawl about "The Kid." I'm over a decade late on Bastion, and that was a little bit of a shame since the game definitely shows its age in a
few spots (particularly enemy readability), but damn if it doesn't fire on all other cylinders. *Build That Wall (Zia's Theme)* has enjoyed
a spot in my work playlist almost every week since I finished the game. Another fantastic short game to plow through in a weekend.

## Transistor
{{< figure
  src="https://images.ctfassets.net/5owu3y35gz1g/CjxdOBzyKsSEimQCwqioI/724624fbfbfa0bcda67c185e1c69d768/1407982466399_Transistor_28-aug-2013_01.jpg?w=1920&q=80"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://www.supergiantgames.com/games/transistor)"
>}}

Finally, I rounded out with Transistor, which many of my friends had described as their favorite of the pre-Hades trilogy. Broken record,
but the art, music, and narrative are enchanting. Transistor didn't really do it for me on the gameplay side of things (I found the way
turn() works to be a bit frustrating), but it has a strong enough foundation that I can look past it. Of the three, it's the one I'm least
likely to replay, but I'm still glad to have experienced it.

I know I'm not saying much about any of the Supergiant lot, but that's because I really think they should be experienced if you haven't
played them. I originally went to college with the intention of getting a degree focused on making video games, but ended up bouncing off of
it into pure computer science for various reasons, not least of which because making a game that really spoke to people felt impossible.
Supergiant, though, is everything I loved about games. Pure passion and artistry from the opening screen to the end credits. It rekindled a
lot of my passion for games. I enjoyed everything I played this year (with only one or two exceptions), and a few of the games I played
(including one coming up) are among my favorites ever. But something about Supergiant is just special in a way that I can't put my finger on
for most other studios. Please play their games.

## Thank Goodness You're Here!
{{< figure
  src="https://thankgoodness-assets.panicfiles.com/tgyh-screenshot-01-market-outside-rons.png"
  caption="[Image Credit](https://thankgoodness.game/)"

I picked this up after seeing it recommended in a couple of places. I've always enjoyed British humor, and the game is basically billed as
"what if absurdist British humor, but for 2 hours and video game." So I picked it up during the Steam winter sale and played through it one
rainy Friday evening.

It's a funny game. I don't think it's the funniest game I've ever played, but the jokes are mostly clever and extremely British, and though
it was starting to fall a bit flat for me around the halfway point, I finished it out and am glad I did. This is the perfect example of a
"exactly what it says on the box" game to me - it's well-written, well-made, entertaining enough to hold your attention, and focuses in on
one thing really well. I probably won't ever touch it again, but I enjoyed my time with it - it's a good one to pick up on sale if you just
want a "turn brain off for a couple hours" chill experience.

## Baldur's Gate 3 :counterclockwise_arrows_button: (kinda)

There are only a few pieces of media that made me cry last year:
* A Man Called Ove - twice
* God of War: Ragnarök - once
* Red Dead Redemption II - once
* The Banshees of Inisherin - once

Baldur's Gate 3 did it five fucking times (final tally: Karlach 2, Gale 1, Astarion 1, Shadowheart 1, Lae'zel very close on a couple
occasions).

I bought BG3 when it first came out in early access and played through a good chunk of Act 1 with my best friends (who also happen to play
D&D with me), and then again all the way through when it fully released (I'm a proud member of the [*Baja Bois* Lane mentions in her video](https://youtu.be/fNI6yWwa8QQ?feature=shared&t=1491)
linked at the top of this post). But for both of those instances, we had a full 4-person party, which completely changes how both the game
and the narrative work. It's still an amazing experience, but a completely different one focused more around doing weird stupid shit and
messing with the game's systems more than its narrative.

I had previously played through most of act 1 on my own just before moving to the Netherlands, but had given away my desktop PC when I left
and never finished any more of the game with that character. So when December rolled around this year and I finally built myself a desktop
that could handle Baldur's Gate (plus, now in ultrawide), I figured I'd sit down and play it through all the way, from the start, with a brand new character.
*Technically* I didn't finish until a couple of weeks into 2025, because the campaign took me almost 70 hours, my intention being to leave
no leaf unturned from my Monk/Warlock elf, Cleo.

Holy fuck, Baldur's Gate 3 is a good game. This should not be new information. It has pretty much universal acclaim from everyone who's ever
touched it. But I have to say it again: ***holy fuck, Baldur's Gate 3 is a good game.*** And playing through it solo, getting to actually
interact with Shadowheart, Gale, Astarion, Lae'zel, Wyll, and of course Karlach out in the world instead of just in camp is just so
completely enthralling that I often didn't put it down for entire weekends. The most impressive part to me is that in that 70 hour
long game (which I have only scratched the surface of) there is **never** a lull in the story that feels like a slog to get through. Every
single quest is fun. Every time you talk to your companions all the way through to the end of act 3 they have something new to say. It's
mind-boggling.

I very intentionally played a goody-two-shoes (with an intimidation proficiency, so a very forceful goody-two-shoes), aiming to get all the
"warm fuzzy" outcomes for the rest of my party. And then the game threw perfect curveballs at me that felt like punches to the gut, so
well timed and well delivered that nothing felt like a fairytale ending, even though I got what could be considered the "good" ending for
everyone. It felt ***satisfying***, which is a really rare trait in games with that many branching pathways, especially one with 70+ hours
worth.

I've already started a new run through as the Dark Urge with one of my friends, fully leaning into the evil route, and even though this is
the 4th time I've played through Act 1 and we're blitzing it, I'm still finding plenty of things I had no idea even existed. The ability to
actually, really adapt the beauty of tabletop D&D to 1s and 0s is jaw-droppingly impressive, and it gives Baldur's Gate 3 a spot on my 10/10
list, no question at all.

Baldur's Gate 3 is a special game. And I leave you with the single funniest line of dialogue I saw in anything all year:

# What I'm Looking at for 2025
I played a LOT of big budget triple A games this year, and I didn't realize it fully until writing this post (by my count, 10 triple-A and 9
indies, counting by budget/production value not by publisher). That's not a bad thing, big budget games have big budgets for a reason - they
pump money into an experience with as many bells and whistles as they can get. A lot of that was because I had just moved to a new country
and was mostly limited to what I could play on my PS5 - but now that I'm lucky enough to have both a Steam Deck and a good desktop, I'd
really like to play through more smaller indies in 2025. I have a lot of things that I want to do this year, and being able to break them up
with some smaller, more tailored experiences is something I'm really looking forward to. I'm sure I'll still end up playing a few of the
newest and greatest too.

Games are really a special form of media. Go out and enjoy some. Thanks for reading.
