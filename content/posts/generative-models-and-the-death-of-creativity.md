# Preface
I'd like to open this by saying that I generally don't like the direction enterprise "AI" is going, at least for consumer-facing
applications. I could (and probably will) write another entire post about the potency some of these models show at spreading misinformation,
or how they're a catalyst for [massive water waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impacts_of_artificial_intelligence#Water_usage) and [huge carbon
footprints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impacts_of_artificial_intelligence#Carbon_footprint).
But that's besides the point of my thoughts down below.
On the whole, there are some instances where I think these new tools are truly a boon to the world. Things like [AlphaFold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlphaFold)
are absolutely incredible applications of this area of technology, and I hope for nothing but their success. This post, however, is specifically around [generative models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_artificial_intelligence), as I've
been slowly watching them creep further and further into two things that I care very deeply about: writing software & tabletop role-playing
games.

# Worlds are Brain-Built
{{< alert "coffee" >}}
One of the best parts about TTRPGs is that the game is truly and entirely **yours** to do with what you please. Play your game however you
want, don't let me or anyone else tell you how to have fun. Now allow me to be a hypocrite.
{{< /alert >}}

I've been playing D&D for about 6 years now, with a couple different groups of friends. For the last 5, I've been the game master for 2
full-length (~2 year long apiece) campaigns, and half of a third, along with numerous one-shots and mini-adventures. For all 5 of those
years, every session has taken place in a homebrew world entirely of my own creation (with the initial catalyst of a pandemic locking me inside
with 3 of my best friends for 2 years). This can either be a very large amount of time or a very very little, depending on who you ask, but
I think it's enough that I've gotten fairly practiced in making (at least the facade of) a world for other people to enjoy. In the last year or
two, starting with [Midjourney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midjourney) and now moving into the million "DM assistant" chat programs, or
the even more joyless [Suno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suno_AI), generative tools have started bleeding their way into the TTRPG space,
offering more and more ways for GMs (and players, to a lesser extent) to create content as quickly as possible. The problem, in my view, is
that this defeats half the fun of TTRPGS.

## The Joy of the Unpredictable
The wiki I've created for my world is a rat's nest. Articles are often a couple of half-baked bullet points with question marks at the end left
for future me to scratch his head at, or a quick table of important info an NPC may or may not know. And I wouldn't want it any other way.
Because it means that I have open ends for myself when I'm actually playing the game. Sure, there are some articles that are filled to the
brim with details and information about made-up cities, people, and artifacts, but do you know how they got that way? My players encountered
one of those bullet-point ridden things and **interacted with it.** And I had to **make something up on the fly from only what little
information I'd set down before.** Now, obviously there are plenty of times during our campaigns where something "scripted" would occur -
some conversation or ambush or grand reveal that I had planned ahead of time or subtly steered the game towards. But even during those
preordained moments, the beauty of TTRPGs is that my players could do, quite literally, anything they wanted in reaction to them. You'll
hear this from any GM you ask, but some of my all-time favorite moments from our games come from times like that. Times where I'd set a
situation in front of my friends fully expecting them to handle it one way, and then they did something so completely out of left field that
I had to improvise a completely new adventure right then and there, while still keeping the overarching story coherent and enjoyable.

So what does any of that have to do with generative AI models? Well, in my opinion, these tools are often marketed and used precisely to
avoid what I just described. If I can plug my tangled, messy wiki into ChatGPT and ask it to come up with an adventure for me, where's the
fun? If the tool spits out an answer to a lore question one of my players has, purely based on the data it's been trained on, that lore
feels disconnected from the rest of the world, because it's likely sourced from the endless amount of fantasy literature the LLM has been
fed. My world is uniquely mine precisely *because* I haven't read every fantasy book in existence - the influences of the stories I have read
and adored can be clearly seen in my own writing, but I've put a spin on it to be something that doesn't exist anywhere else. And that's the
fundamental issue to me, at least with GM "writing assistants:" you're taking away the personality that comes from off-the-cuff
improvisation in favor of vomitting as much generic information onto the page as you can. Embracing the mistakes, the personal quirks that
you and your players bring to the table, is what gives the game its magic & soul.

## Magic and Soul
Now, text models aside, I will be the first to admit that I actually tried one of the image generators for a few days during the start of
our current campaign (specifically I used Bing create as it had a generous free token system). One of our group has [aphantasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphantasia),
and I figured it might be an interesting way to give him visuals for some of the more important locations I was describing, along with
giving me a little bit of inspiration when adding detail to the wiki later. At first it seemed really neat, the stuff it spat out was
definitely a bit "same-y" in style and a little too grandiose for what I was aiming for, but it really got me in the mood to worldbuild.

The problem was, after about a week of sprinkling articles here and there with some of these images, in particular a page for a very
important location that the party would soon be visiting, I realized that I'd lost my initial mental image of the place in favor of the
not-quite-right jpeg that Bing had spat out a few days before. And I hated it. This place was supposed to be a dark sanatorium in the middle
of a cold, foreboding lake - I had tons of horror-themed story threads set up and ready to go for it. But the image, while not bad in
its own right, just didn't *quite* hit that vibe - it was too clean, too much like something you'd see in a Disney movie. And I hated that I
had lost that first mental picture of Ravenhead, sitting atop the black rocks, rising towards the clouded moon with a sense of doom about
it. When the party did reach it, I actually felt that my storytelling fell a bit flat, as I had more trouble than usual describing its
appearance, and I couldn't help but wonder if I had ruined their own imaginations just that little bit by providing them with a visual
reference for a thing that should exist only in the mind's eye. In the same way that the Hogwarts I imagined the first time I read Harry
Potter is completely unique from how anyone else pictured it, I was missing that extra touch of magic that brings tabletop games to life.

It was also around this time that I had started dating a woman who is one of the most talented artists I have ever met, and talking
with her was really illuminating about how these image generators work. I knew the general technical background, but until she started
pointing out things that only model-generated images have (or lack), I hadn't really been able to put my finger on what was "off" about
them. Somewhat obviously, they're missing that underlying sense of human-ness to them. One blends into the next into the next, even if
they've been prompted to mimic different artistic styles. And I think that's a detriment to the wonderful part about TTRPGs: every single
table has its own, unique, **human** style that makes it a one-of-a-kind experience. The mind's eye (even for someone with aphantasia) just
can't be matched.

# Code
I don't think it's unreasonable to say that I probably spend a good 50% of my time awake either reading, writing, or thinking about code.
Not only am I a software engineer by trade, but I enjoy tinkering with and occasionally building open-source software in my free time. I am
fortunate enough to have graduated in 2021, just before ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot were released. Obviously, I still pursued a computer
science degree in the age of the Internet, so it wasn't as if I couldn't find assistance (or even full solutions) for my classes, but I
think there's a key difference in the fact that I had to actually build an understanding of the clockwork to use those things effectively,
rather than just knowing how to use a clock.

## Clock vs. Clockwork
Now, you can make a fairly similar argument for this analogy with StackOverflow or just finding code on GitHub/GitLab, etc. To use an
extremely trivial example, if I want to find code for the DFS algorithm, I can find tens of thousands of examples in basically every single language
imaginable. For foundational concepts like this, of course you're able to find answers without an LLM assist. The difference comes with
projects I had in my upper-level classes, towards the end of my degree. The one that sticks most clearly in my mind is building my own
memory allocator for a systems programming class. Again, there are thousands of examples of this out there on the internet. The difference,
though, is that none of them are *exactly* the same as the parameters my professor set for ours. They may use different allocation
strategies, or have different constraints, etc. And there's *just enough* variance there that I had to "understand the clockwork" as it
were. I couldn't write a memory allocator without understanding, at least to some baseline level, what I was actually trying to achieve.

With things like Copilot and Devin though, I could plug the text of the assignment into the model and have it spit out an answer that
exactly matched what I needed in seconds. And then I could run it against the tests I was given (without writing any more of my own of
course, why would I do that when the model obviously made no mistakes), see that they passed, and nod to myself having "read the clock." And
by doing that I would have done a pretty nasty disservice to myself. That example is pretty tailored towards college specifically, but you
can extend the same thing to the professional workplace: If you're writing an authentication system, and you don't know how it works but
just slap in some code ChatGPT gave you, I guarantee you've written a security flaw. You may have written one yourself if you didn't use
ChatGPT, but the difference is that you understand the clockwork you used and will be able to find and fix the bug significantly faster.

I want to be clear that I don't think LLM code assistants are a bad thing in and of themselves. But they, like every other piece of new
technology, are a **tool**, not a silver bullet. There are no silver bullets. I've seen code assistants be incredibly helpful to coworkers
and friends, especially for generating repetitive, boilerplate code. I also don't think it's wrong to use them when you're still learning
how to write software. But the key, just like with StackOverflow or some random person's GitHub, is to actually read the code that the tool
gave you, and understand what the clockwork is doing before you turn it into a clock.

## The More Dumb Things You Do, The Fewer Dumb Things You Do Again
I make a lot of stupid mistakes (in life in general, but especially when I write code). I think it's reasonable to say that most software
engineers do. Sometimes those mistakes rear their heads immediately, and sometimes they're a foot-gun that sits biding its time for months
only to make future me hate past me. But you know what always happens when I find a dumb mistake or decision past me made? I fix it. And
usually (not always, but usually) I can fix it pretty fast, because I'm the one that did the dumb thing in the first place, and I know
better than anyone how my brain works. And anecdotally, that means I am far less likely to make the same stupid mistake again in the
future, and I'm more likely to take a lesson away from it in the present.

When Copilot/ChatGPT/whatever makes a mistake though, I have no insight onto why it did the stupid thing that it did. This is a very broken record take that
has been made by a multitude of far smarter and more experienced people than I, but in the handful of times that I have tried an LLM code
assistant (I messed with [GitLab's](https://about.gitlab.com/solutions/code-suggestions/) briefly in early 2024), I spent so much time undoing
the code that it generated and re-writing it to work the way I needed that I should have just written it myself in the first place. And the
occasion that it did write code that I kept, when I started testing I found places where it just wasn't handling edge cases properly. Am I
saying I would have handled those edge cases properly out of the gate? Absolutely not. What I'm saying is that if I had been the one to
write the incorrect code, it would have taken me half the time to correct it than it did to read through what the LLM had generated, parse
what it was doing, and figure out what it missed. In many ways it reminded me of my time as a teaching assistant in college, for the most
introductory programming class offered. Students would write code that, while syntactically correct, made all kinds of absolutely bizzare
decisions. The difference is that my students got better when they asked for help, because *they fixed their own code*.

# The Death of Creativity
One of the most sadly ironic things about the absolute torrent of LLM tools is that they're doing the exact opposite of what they were
promised to be. Many of these things are marketed as "it does the boring work so you can be creative!" But when you really look at it, or at
least when I do, I tend to find that the tool is taking away all the creative fun in the spirit of getting it done faster or with more
(sloppy) detail. ***I don't think all LLMs are inherently bad, nor do I think they shouldn't be used.*** My opinion, as I said up above, is
that all of these things are tools, and they're tools with drawbacks that aren't as immediately obvious. Don't let the novelty of the tool
take away the joy of creating.
