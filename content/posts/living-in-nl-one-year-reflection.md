{{< alert "coffee" >}}
It's been just over a year since I moved to the Netherlands, and I've been feeling like a reflection is in order. I'm extremely lucky and
grateful to be able to live abroad - this post is just various musings about my first year in the land of windmills and bikes.
{{< /alert >}}

My employer opened an office in Amsterdam in early 2023, and I took it as an opportunity to say "fuck it, we ball" and moved to the
Netherlands, arriving at the start of December 2023. I got extremely lucky - they were willing to go along with the move, I'm from the
U.S. so I had very few immigration issues, and I work in tech, meaning those few bumps I normally would have had on the immigration process
were virtually nonexistent.

# Moving
Strangely, the actual process of moving was the least stressful part of the whole thing for me (at least in terms of planning and actual
execution, I was absolutely scared shitless as the day approached). The last two weeks of November 2024 were spent giving away things I
wasn't taking with me to friends and coworkers and packing most of the rest into a few small cardboard boxes. Those, along with 3 suitcases
full of things I would be bringing with me across the Atlantic and an old Trek road bike I had borrowed from my dad, were loaded into the
back of my car and driven for 12 hours from Salt Lake City to Tucson. The process of slimming down to just the things I cared about was
surprisingly satisfying. Seeing everything I care about packed into a single trunk was a surreal experience (and one that I absolutely could
not replicate unless I was a single early 20-something).

After a little under a week in Tucson with my parents, saying goodbye and enjoying my last good Mexican food for a while (more on that
later), I drove to Phoenix where I returned the car to my Grandparents (who I had been renting it from) and boarded a flight at Sky Harbor
that would take me (ironically) back through SLC and then across the Atlantic to Schipol. I was certainly nervous at this point, but I was
surprised at how calm things were once I was actually on the plane(s). At some level, the lead-up was where I had all of my convictions
challenged and overthought everything, once I was in the air it felt like it was just time to get on with it. I landed in the morning on the
last day of November and found my way to the hotel I was staying at without much issue.

For those of you unaware, the housing market in the Netherlands is, it put it mildly, actively doused in petrol and set ablaze. It is hard
to find a place to live, especially in one of the big cities. So, I was immensely fortunate to find an apartment within 3 weeks of moving.
Now, I had opted to pay a relocation company to help me out with this - especially the logistics of handling my residence permit
appointments and all of the bureaucracy that had to happen while I was jetlagged (they are called [Jimble](https://jimble.nl/) and were
absolutely worth every penny). That is a luxury that I am extremely lucky to have been able to afford. A theme throughout this entire
reflection will be that many things were easier than I thought (at least, the logistical things) and I think it's only fair to acknowledge
that a lot of that is because I'm an expat from a wealthy country, here of my own volition, with a well-paying job. I also just got lucky,
especially with the apartment, which I moved into the week of Christmas, and finally took a breath ready to settle in.

# Day to Day
## Cycling

{{< alert "coffee" >}}
If you're interested in why US cities are so terrible for everything except cars, I encourage you to check out [Not Just
Bikes](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UC0intLFzLaudFG-xAvUEO-A) on YouTube, particularly [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=REni8Oi1QJQ).
{{< /alert >}}

Bikes kick ass. I grew up in Tucson, one of the most cycling-friendly cities in the U.S. I spent a lot of my teenage years on a bike,
especially with my dad and grandfather, who are both avid cyclists. And I had visited Amsterdam once, as a tourist when I was 17, and rode
around a little in the city then. So I thought when I moved I could just find a crappy omafiets (grandma bike) for sale and hop on with no
issues. This was an incorrect assumption.

Ok, it wasn't really **that** bad. It's just a completely different type of cycling when:
1. Everything is built around bike infrastructure (amazing)
2. Bikes are an actual means of transport in addition to being for sport (amazing)
3. Everyone else knows the rules of the road and you don't (though some of them choose to ignore it. If you ride one of the fat-wheel
   electric bikes that are basically motorcycles you are automatically labeled an asshole in my mind)

So I was a little scattered for the first month or so, especially since it was the start of winter, which means everyone is as bundled up on
their bikes as they can get.

But holy shit, now that I've been here over a year I cannot imagine living in a city without a bike again. I know it's unfair to compare
Amsterdam (a pancake-flat, relatively compact European city with infrastructure designed and built over decades for bikes) to a US city,
even one with "good" cycling friendliness like Tucson, but man is the US behind on this one. I ride my bike about 3.5km (~2 miles for the
freedom unit readers) to and from the metro station to go into the office most weekdays, and that 15-20 minutes (assuming it's not raining)
is such a fantastic way to start my morning or end my evening. I can ride my shitty, rusty, clanking 90 euro pile of bolts anywhere in the
city in under 45 minutes if I really need to. That might sound like a lot (and for trips over 5k I usually opt for the bus or the
streetcar), but when you really think about it that's about the same amount of time it would take in a car in rush hour in any good-sized
American city, if not longer. And in the car you'd be trapped bumper to bumper for large stretches, rather than in constant motion.

Bike (and pedestrian, can't forget that you can walk everywhere without fear of being run over by a truck the size of an elephant)-centric
infrastructure is the future. It won't work everywhere, but when cities are designed to be **livable** for people rather than optimized for
cars, it makes life so much better.

## Public Transport
Trains kick ass. Reliable, well-funded, easy to use public transport in general is a breath of absolute fresh air in comparison to both of
the cities I've lived in the U.S. - and to be honest in comparison to a lot of western cities Salt Lake and Tucson both have pretty "decent"
options for public transport, at least in the major areas.

There's a weird prevailing thought pattern I've observed in the States that public transport is only around for people who don't have a car,
but that the goal really should be to get your own car so that you're no longer "reliant" on public transportation. Which is an incredibly
stupid way of looking at it. A bus or tram or metro can get to places that no car could, and it can do it without requiring 90% of the space
to be dedicated to a parking lot. I can get **anywhere** in Amsterdam in under an hour on public transport (or in combination with my bike/a
short walk), and it often takes even less than that to get to a completely different city. I used to go to Utrecht at least once a week, and
regardless of if I was leaving from home or work, I could be at Utrecht Centraal in 40 minutes or less.

Again, there's a consideration here about the Netherlands as a country - flat, relatively small, and with centuries of existing established
pathways between settlements to build on. But even still, let me put in perspective what trains (even non-high-speed ones) could do:
* The distance between Amsterdam and Brussels is ~200km (120mi). That's about the same as the distance between Tucson and Phoenix. The
Eurostar train from Amsterdam to Brussels is around 1 hour 52 minutes, which is about the same as the drive from Tucson to Phoenix (having
done it many times myself), especially once you factor in the traffic.
* The distance between Amsterdam and Paris is ~520km (310 mi). That's about 80km (50mi) less than the distance between Phoenix and Los
Angeles via I-10. The Eurostar train from Amsterdam to Paris takes roughly **3 hours and 20 minutes.** That's in comparison to an **8+ hour
drive** between Phoenix and LA, two of the biggest cities in the western US. Even if you pessimistically account for that last 50 mile
stretch, the train still takes half the time or less while carrying 200x more people.

I could run more of these examples, and sure the spacing between major US cities (especially on the west coast) tends to be larger than
Europe, but my point still stands. America is brainwashed by road trip culture. Getting where I'm going comfortably, while being able to do
something other than stare at a stretch of asphalt for hours on end, is infinitely better than sitting behind the wheel. Cars still have a
use, and there are plenty of places here where having one is absolutely useful, but the priority should run: pedestrian/bike friendly > Good
public transport > car infrastructure, not the other way around. 

***Public transport good, giant parking lots bad. Thank you for coming to my TED Talk.***

## Necessities
Abundant, easily accessible, high quality groceries kick ass. Again, there's some privilege here living in a wealthy country and being able
to afford what I need without much sweat. That said, I will never go back to the practice of buying all my groceries for the week. Being
able to stop in at (by my quick count) 6 different grocery stores on the ride between my apartment and the office, plus having at least 3
more within easy walking distance of home, means that I buy food one or two days at a time, *maybe* for a whole weekend. This results in a
couple things that I love:
1. I don't ever waste food, because it never goes bad.
2. I tend to eat healthier (not only because junk food isn't as subsidized as the US), because if I'm buying fresh produce I'm going to use
   it immediately.
3. I cook more. Eating out is expensive, paying 5-8 euro for dinner ingredients that will have leftovers for a couple days is way better
   than paying 15-20 for a roti and fries (maybe bad example, Surinamese food is fucking delicious).

This is true of things other than groceries too. The Netherlands definitely has some of that corporate ubiquitesness that's ever-present in
the US, but the difference is that there's almost always at least one smaller local place within 100 meters of your Actions or Hemas. It's
not perfect (and to some degree the big stores are unavoidable), but I do like being able to go to a little local shop on a whim rather than
making it my targeted destination.

# Woes
## Worst 42k Square Kilometers of Mexican Food
Tucson is known locally as "The Best 23 Miles Of Mexican Food" (at least Sonoran-style Mexican food). I've had a trading card for my favorite
taco shop in my wallet for almost a decade now (it's called [BK](https://bktacos.com/) and if you're ever in Tucson should go to the top of
your list). Tucson is also [classified as a "world city of gastronomy"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucson,_Arizona#Cuisine) - just in
general the food scene there is off the charts good. Salt Lake was also no pushover; one of the beautiful things of the U.S. being such a
melting pot of cultures is that you get the opportunity to experience so many diverse things, food among them.

To my Dutch friends, I'm sorry: your food sucks. Your favorite flavor is "brown". You rule the world on the cheese front and then have
skated by on that fame for centuries.

Amsterdam (and really anywhere in the Randstad) are big and diverse enough that there's plenty of good food to be found (Surinamese in
particular), but it stumbles at the last hurdle for me in comparison particularly to Tucson. Ragging on Dutch food is a well-known expat
thing, so I'll keep it brief - it has made me really appreciate the beauty of all of the cuisine diversity in the U.S.

## The Sun is an Honored Guest
I will be honest, the worst part of the Netherlands (and the part that's unavoidable) is the winter. I lived in SLC for almost 7 years, I'm
quite comfortable in the cold (despite growing up in the Sonoran Desert), and I don't mind winter snow/rain much either. Unless the sky is
cold and grey for weeks on end.

I've been here through a winter and a half now, and particularly this winter there have been stretches of 10+ days where I literally did not
see the sun. Say what you will about temperature or precipitation, at least in SLC the sun never stopped shining for more than a handful of
days. Even while I'm writing this, I'm glancing out the window of my apartment at a slate-grey wall of cloud that probably won't change all
day. That shit **sucks.**

The summer is the opposite of course - 16 hours of daylight at the perfect temperature. But the winter more than anything has been the
biggest struggle to adjust to for me, though I think I'm finally over that hump.

## I Miss Running Uphill
I'm quite an avid runner. It's been my sport of choice for a few years, and especially in SLC I got deep into the trail running scene.
Slowly grinding away at a steep trail among the trees and the quiet of the mountains, looking around to see the valley spread out behind
you, tearing back down that same trail on your way home, that's one of the places where I'm happiest. I love mountains.

My strava recap from 2024 says that my elevation gain was down ***87%*** (13214 meters in 2023 -> 1731 meters in 2024, my first year here). In **ONE** event in 2023, I did 577m
of elevation gain over 12.17km. It took me *almost 3 months* to do that same elevation gain while I was in my heaviest training load last
summer - **and half of it was when I was visiting Salt Lake City for vacation.**

So yeah, I miss the mountains.

# Ik Woon Hier
To be honest, around October/November of 2024 (just as I was coming up on the exact one year mark) I was planning to move back to the U.S. I
just felt like I was missing too many familiar things, and wanted to go back to what I was comfortable with.

But something changed over the next month or two (my country electing a felon didn't help its case), and even though this winter has been
pretty miserable by all accounts, something about the Netherlands started to feel like... home. Plenty of things are still new and
unfamiliar in Dutch (and European as a whole) culture, but I feel comfortable here in a way that I didn't for that first year. I've started
taking Dutch classes to integrate more, and with some recent opportunities I've had to actually sit down and think about if this is the
place I want to live for the forseeable future. And honestly, barring a major event, I think it is. I don't know if it's the place I want to
spend the rest of my life (that requires mountains), but it feels like somewhere that I actually want to make a part of my life rather than
just being another expat guest. ***Ik woon hier nu en ik ben gelukkig.***
