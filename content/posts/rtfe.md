# Read The Fucking Error
That's the tagline. The age old, often needlessly aggressive ["Read The Fucking
Manual"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RTFM) (or "Friendly" if you don't have a
sailor's mouth), but with a fresh new spin. I'm truly a modern Socrates.

Seriously though, you have to read the error. There's a variety of [well-written
discourse](https://compassionatecoding.com/blog/2019/4/17/its-time-to-retire-rtfm)
about RTFM, and having spent a lot of time as a teaching assistant in college I
tend to personally lean towards the view that "RTFM" is often
counter-productive. There's a very fine line to walk between trying to help
someone help themselves and being a dick, and generally I think that *most* of
the instances RTFM is thrown around are the latter.

So why, then, am I writing a blog post basically encouraging the same thing but
with a different word at the end? Well, there's one important qualifier for me
here (which is not in the tagline): I am talking in the context of software
development (either professional or open source), and I am ***very
specifically*** talking about contexts where it can safely be assumed that the
person not reading the error should be capable of doing so and understanding it.

A few examples:
* RTFE makes sense when:
    1. The person asking about the error has at least a couple of years of
       software development experience under their belt.
    2. The error in question is unabashedly clear. As an example, here's a rust
       compilation error if you provide the wrong number of arguments to a
    function (again, see 1 above - someone with at least a year or two of
    software experience should absolutely know what all of these words mean):
        ```rust
        error[E0061]: this function takes 2 arguments but 1 argument was supplied
        --> src/main.rs:61:5
           |
        61 |     foo(1);
           |     ^^^--- argument #2 of type `usize` is missing
           |
        note: function defined here
          --> src/main.rs:58:4
           |
        58 | fn foo(a: usize, b: usize) {}
           |    ^^^ --------  --------
        help: provide the argument
           |
        61 |     foo(1, /* usize */);
           |        ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

        ```
    3. The error-causing code has been very obviously generated by an LLM
       (assuming that the engineer not R-ing the FE is the one who generated
    it).
* RTFE *doesn't* make sense when:
    1. The person asking about the error is new to software development
       altogether, or highly inexperienced.
    2. The error message is poorly written (which definitely happens, having read and
       written many terrible error messages myself).
    3. Some other reasonable barrier to understanding it exists - the person
       doesn't speak the language of the error as their first language, for
    example.

## How To RTFE
Ok, so I've now gotten my complaining out of the way. Old man can now put his
fist down and try to be helpful: here's what I've found to be the most helpful
when I'm actually debugging and I hit something like this. This is how my brain
works, so YMMV, but I'm willing to bet it's at least relatable to most engineers.

1. **Read the error twice. Or more.** Sometimes (like the rust example above),
   the error in question is pretty immediately understandable. The majority of
the time, though, especially in the case of compilation errors, even extremely
specific errors have a lot to parse. So read it, and then read it again if you
didn't immediately slap your head and go "oh, duh."
2. **If it includes a source reference, go look at it.** Again, this is mostly
   relevant for compilation errors or runtime exceptions that include a stack
trace, but you have to go get the context. Especially if you're working in a
new/unfamiliar codebase, or just with code that you didn't write, you won't have
any blinding flashes of insight because you don't know what's happening. You
*have to* get that context.
3. **Look at other pieces of context, if they're available.** Does the thing
   you're developing against have logs or other sources of observability you can
   look at that might provide information about what was happening leading up to
   the error? Is there documentation somewhere with a "troubleshooting" section
   or similar that might include the problem? This doesn't just mean "google it
   lol," it means that you need to build an understanding of what happened and
why.
4. **Try something. Probably try multiple things.** If a solution isn't
   immediately obvious, try something. Maybe the error message gave you a recommendation
   (again, the rust compiler is a great example here). Maybe you have an inkling
   of what the problem is, but you're not sure. Maybe the web API you're hitting
   gave you a `400 Bad Request` - ok, what happens if you try submitting
slightly different data? Remember the golden rule: a *new* error means you're
making progress, because now you have more information to work with.
5. **Once you've done all of that, ask for help** ***with information.*** I want
   to make sure this is really clear: there is absolutely nothing wrong with
asking for assistance, nor is there such a thing as a stupid question. But those
two statements are only true if you make an effort to give the person(s) you're
asking information to help you. If you send me a message that says *"hey, I got a
`400 Bad Requst`"* and zero other information, I'm gonna want to hit you with a
RTFE. If instead you send me *"hey, I hit `/api/foo/bar` with this blob of data
and it gave me a `400 Bad Request - <error message>`. I tried again
with this different blob of data and got the same thing, is there something I'm
doing wrong?"* I'm much, much more likely to both want to and be able to help
you.

## How to WA(G)FE
All of this is contingent on the error giving useful and workable information.
Unfortunately, much as it would be fantastic if this was always the case, it
definitely is not. There's no perfect formula for the "perfect" error message -
it's highly dependent on so many things, for example:
* Is it an error that must be immediately resolved (like a compilation error)
for the code to even run?
* Does the error have access to a stack trace or source context?
* Can this error only be reached by a specific set of bad paths and there's a
limited number of valid ways to avoid it?
* Does the error need to be sanitized so that it doesn't reveal information
about the inner working of the system for security reasons?
* And many more.

I am more than culpable in writing a large number of errors that aren't great.
Every software developer who has ever or will ever live is (and anyone who
claims the errors from their library are perfect is a liar). So lets talk about
some ways to Write a (Good) Fucking Error in the first place.

First and foremost, in my experience, is including as much information as is
reasonable. That's a bit of a non-statement since that's such a wide net to
cast, but I think it's one that can be learned and practiced. There's a balance
between telling the user what went wrong and flooding them with information, but
I personally tend to find that verbosity (as long as it's not undecipherable
garbage) is better than terseness. My rule of thumb is to include at least these
3 pieces of information:
1. **What precisely the program could not handle.**
2. **Where exactly the problem happened.**
3. **If relevant, what data was given that resulted in the bad path.**

Again, pretty broad, but lets use that example from the rust compiler again:
```rust
error[E0061]: this function takes 2 arguments but 1 argument was supplied
--> src/main.rs:61:5
   |
61 |     foo(1);
   |     ^^^--- argument #2 of type `usize` is missing
   |
note: function defined here
  --> src/main.rs:58:4
   |
58 | fn foo(a: usize, b: usize) {}
   |    ^^^ --------  --------
help: provide the argument
   |
61 |     foo(1, /* usize */);
   |        ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

```
The output is pretty clear about point (1) - the function call takes exactly 2
arguments, but it was only given 1. It's also pretty helpful on point (2): the
error happened on line 61 of `src/main.rs`, and it even gives us the line where
the function is defined as a bonus. And again, for point (3), the compiler shows
us the bad call: `foo(1)` and even provides a "help" chunk at the bottom showing
us a possible solution (give it the other argument).

This is a simple example, and this is a case where all of that information is
available with no restrictions. But anecdotally, I've found that cases like this
account for most of the errors you encounter on a day to day basis. Web APIs in
particular may need to obfuscate some of this, for example I would never expect
a `400 Bad Request` to give me a stack trace from where in the source code I'm
getting that error, but a lot of that can be mitigated with [good observability
practices](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/observability-primer/).

Sometimes it's not easy to WAGFE. I get that. But it is your responsibility as
the author of code that someone else might have to read in the future to try and
give what information you can. And, if you do that, you'll more than likely have
more to work with when someone inevitably comes asking having not RTFE.

## Being Blunt and Being a Dick
Again, I wanna make sure that it's clear I'm not advocating replying to bug
reports with "RTFE" and nothing else. We've all been in the situation of trying
to decipher an error and just having nothing click. It can be frustrating to
both be that person, and to be the person who gets a bug report with minimal
effort put in. I get it, I've been both people. So to echo the excellent
Compassionate Coding post above:

> The compassionate alternative is to make the independent problem-solving less
> painful.
>
>  — <cite>April Wensel</cite>

Ask follow-up questions, but don't feel bad saying "I need more information to
help you solve this." I find this to be especially effective with junior
engineers or folks who tend to consistently ask the same/similar questions:
you're not brushing them off, but you're encouraging them to hone their own
debugging skills first. Occasionally, if I have a good inkling of what the
problem might be off of that first message, I'll provide a couple things that
might be good to try, but always ending with something to the effect of "that is
not a complete list of things to try, please try those things in addition to any
other information you can give me."

I think the last thing that's important to remember is that we're all people.
It's really easy to get frustrated with annoying, empty bug reports, just like
it's easy to get frustrated with obtuse and unhelpful error messages. But both
sides of that coin are another intelligent person - take a breath before you
let the frustration overwhelm you. There's nothing wrong with venting about it
if you need to, but it pays dividends to not be a dick.
